Task 5: Simulated data

(a) Changing orientation uncertainty

Even with constant sensor noise, the orientation uncertainty can change. The
ESKF's error dynamics change with orientation, acceleration and angular
velocity. Its GNSS matrix changes with orientation through the lever arm. When
the drone manoeuvres, the position measurements give different amounts of
information about orientation and biases. The uncertainty therefore doesn't
have to settle at one value. It also changes between predictions and GNSS
updates. Figure 1 shows the orientation estimates, and Figure 2 shows the
errors and uncertainty.

(b) Yaw error and divergence

Roll and pitch errors tilt the estimated gravity direction. This affects the
predicted motion, which GNSS can help correct. Gravity doesn't tell me the
heading, so yaw is harder to estimate. This explains the larger yaw error and
uncertainty in Figure 2.

Without heading information, gyro noise and bias can make yaw drift and its
estimated uncertainty keep growing. Standing still or flying straight at
constant velocity gives little information to correct this. Losing GNSS also
makes it harder. Manoeuvres with GNSS available can help limit the drift.
The heading can keep drifting, even though the plotted angle wraps around at
plus or minus 180 degrees. Large errors also break the ESKF's assumption of
small errors.

(c) Rounded calibration matrices

I ran the first 300 seconds with the original and rounded matrices, keeping
the other settings the same. Both rounded matrices became identity matrices.
Position vector RMSE went from 0.46 m to 0.70 m, velocity vector RMSE from
0.19 m/s to 0.44 m/s, and yaw-direction error RMSE from 0.43 to 1.99 degrees.
I checked errors every 0.1 seconds and after GNSS updates.

Rounding removes the small scale and alignment corrections. This leaves
systematic IMU errors that build up during integration. GNSS limits position
drift, but doesn't measure orientation directly. Estimated biases can make up
for some calibration error, but not fully for errors that change with
acceleration and angular velocity.

The filter also became overconfident. Its average orientation standard
deviations barely changed, even though the actual errors grew. Mean 15-state
NEES went from 20.7 to 1692.7. Figures 2 and 3 show the orientation errors
before and after rounding. Figure 4 shows the orientation NEES after rounding.

(d) Tuning experiments

I tested each change over 300 seconds with original calibration. Baseline
position and velocity RMSEs were 0.46 m and 0.19 m/s, with mean NIS 2.99 and
full-state NEES 20.7.

1. I reduced gnss_std_ne from 0.3 to 0.03 m and gnss_std_d from 0.5 to 0.05
m. Position and velocity RMSE rose to 0.64 m and 0.39 m/s, with mean NIS 247.2
and NEES 564.1. The filter trusted the same noisy measurements too much,
making the estimates worse and overconfident.

2. I increased accm_std and gyro_std by a factor of ten, to 0.01167 and
0.000436 respectively. Position and velocity RMSE barely changed, at 0.47 m
and 0.19 m/s. Mean NEES fell to 15.8, close to the expected value of 15, and
NIS was 2.93. The uncertainty better matched the errors, but the estimates
weren't more accurate.

3. I set gnss_min_dt_sim to 10 seconds, reducing GNSS updates from 299 to 30.
Position and velocity RMSE rose to 3.16 m and 0.66 m/s as IMU errors built up
between corrections. Mean NIS was still near 3 at 3.25, so a reasonable NIS
doesn't necessarily mean small position errors.

Task 6: Real data

(a) GNSS between IMU timestamps

I can split the prediction at the GNSS timestamp: predict to that time,
update and inject, then predict through the rest of the IMU interval. The loop
already does this, using the same IMU sample for both parts and assuming
constant input. If GNSS arrives after the filter has passed its timestamp, I
can update a saved state at that time and rerun the later predictions and
updates.

(b) Rounded calibration matrices

Over 200-600 seconds, the rounded and original runs differed by 0.27 m RMS in
position. Local orientation differences were 1.37, 2.80 and 1.49 degrees RMS.
These compare the two estimates, not their errors against ground truth. Mean
position NIS rose from 3.65 to 4.25, and the fraction inside the 95% interval
fell from 86% to 83% (Figures 5 and 6).

Without ground truth, I couldn't tell from these changes alone that the
calibration was wrong. GNSS keeps position in check, and estimated biases can
hide some calibration errors. NIS points to worse consistency, but doesn't
tell me how accurate the orientation is or what caused the problem. For real
use, I need good calibration and tests with different manoeuvres, preferably
with an independent reference.

(c) Tuning without ground truth

I tested each change over 200-600 seconds with original calibration and
baseline mean position NIS 3.65.

1. Reducing gnss_std_ne and gnss_std_d to 0.03 and 0.05 m raised NIS to 139.4
(Figure 7), even though RMS innovation fell from 0.96 to 0.80 m. Following
GNSS more closely didn't prove better accuracy: the filter was too confident.
Agreement with noisy measurements isn't enough to judge accuracy.

2. Increasing these standard deviations to 3 and 5 m reduced NIS to 0.34
(Figure 8), while RMS innovation rose to 2.80 m. The larger assumed covariance
makes NIS smaller without fixing the model. I therefore can't tune just by
trying to get the lowest NIS; I still need independent checks.

3. Setting gnss_min_dt_real to 10 seconds reduced updates from 400 to 40. Mean
NIS reached 71.7, mostly because of large innovations at the start (Figure 9),
although 80% of updates were inside the 95% interval. Fewer corrections made
it harder for the filter to settle. Reasonable innovations later on don't rule
out errors between updates, which are hard to assess without ground truth.
